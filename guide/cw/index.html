    CW (beta)¶

    To open the CW page, open the page picker (swipe right from the left edge, or tap the left grip) and tap MODE: CW. The spectrum and waterfall keep running behind it, so you still see the band while you operate. The page ships on and is a beta. Turn it off with cw_page_enabled = no in a config file, or {"action":"cw_enable","on":false} over the API, if you would rather not have it yet.

    !!! warning "There is no abort for a message in progress" KY has no abort in the QMX's CAT manual. Tested on the bench: neither sending RX; nor bouncing the mode away from CW and back stops an in-flight send - both were tried and both simply had no effect, the keyer ran to completion either way. Once the radio holds a message, it sends to the end. What you can stop is text that is still queued on the Tab5 - see STOP. Keying the radio itself is confirmed working, off air, by the operator.

    The page is built for one-handed, gloved, field operation — the kind of CW you do standing at a picnic table with a paddle you have not touched in a month.

    (v1.25.0) The CW page is now also on the web — see Web UI → CW Control for sending macros, editing them, switching macro sets, entering a callsign by hand, and setting a contest name, all from a browser.

    The screen¶

    Left pane, top to bottom:

    Control What it does
    MODE: CW Header. The page forces the radio into CW mode whenever you enter it.
    WPM with - / + Keyer speed, read back from the radio so the number always matches what the QMX itself thinks. Tap the number itself (v1.24.2) for a numeric keypad instead of stepping one WPM at a time.
    Park Your POTA park reference, used by the {PARK} macro. Tap to edit.
    TX offset (v1.24.2) Opens the CW transmit offset popup — see TX offset below.
    NEXT The one-tap QSO ladder — see Running a QSO.
    Six memory buttons Your keyer messages.
    Status line What the radio is doing: receiving, keying, or how many characters are left to send. While text is queued it is replaced by a red STOP button (v1.26.0).
    General / Contest toggle (bottom of the panel) Switches between two named sets of six macros — see Macro sets. Placed at the very bottom, separated from the memory buttons by the status line, so it cannot be mis-hit while working the macros above it.

    Right of the pane: the live spectrum and waterfall, the decode ticker along the bottom, the copilot hint line just above it, and the station card when a callsign has been heard.

    Band discipline¶

    The CW page lets you operate the CW and Digi portions of the band — not the phone portion. That is deliberate: CW is legal inside the digital sub-band, but digital modes are not legal inside the CW one, so clamping to CW alone would deny you spectrum you are actually allowed to use.

    • Entering the page moves you into the band's CW segment if you were somewhere else.
    • Changing band lands you in the middle of that band's CW segment.
    • Tuning — by tap, drag, band-plan strip or keypad, on the Tab5 or from the web (v1.25.0 — the web's own tuning paths now honour this too) — is clamped to CW + Digi. You cannot tune into the phone portion while on this page.
    • The band-plan strip below the spectrum draws the CW and Digi segments only, each in its own colour, stretched to fill the whole strip — never Phone — and the filter is set to 300 Hz on entry.
    • (v1.25.0) Spots on this page are CW only, even though Digi is in reach — see Live Spots. Tapping an FT8/FT4 activity spot would switch the radio's mode away from CW, which this page has no business offering.

    The segment boundaries come from the band plan for your region, which you can set in Settings → Band-plan region.

    TX offset¶

    (v1.24.2) Tap the TX offset button, beside Park, to open a popup with four nudge buttons (-50, -10, +10, +50 Hz) and the current value.

    This shifts where you transmit relative to where you are listening — without moving your dial. It exists so you can avoid zero-beating the exact frequency of the station you are calling: many operators prefer to be heard a little off to the side of a pileup rather than dead on top of it. The radio achieves this with its own split (VFO A/B) mechanism under the hood; you do not need to think about VFOs, only about the offset in Hz.

    • Clear resets the offset to zero (transmit where you listen — "zero-beat"). This is a different thing from the long-press on the top bar's BW indicator, which retunes your receive dial onto a signal's peak — the two happen to share the word "zero-beat" in amateur radio but do opposite jobs: one moves your RX frequency onto a signal, the other clears a TX/RX offset back to none.
    • Range is ±300 Hz, in 10 Hz steps from the nudge buttons.
    • The setting is stored on the Tab5 and survives a reboot; it has no effect outside CW/CW-R.
    • This same control also exists in the settings drawer (Settings → Radio → CW TX offset) if you prefer a slider.

    Filter bandwidth¶

    The top bar's BW: --- indicator is live in CW mode as well as FT8/SSB/AM: tap it for the same filter-width popup you use everywhere else, showing only the widths your radio's own CW → Choose filters menu has enabled. There is no separate bandwidth control on the CW page itself — the top bar one already works here, and reaches the radio's real hardware filter.

    Break-in delay (Semi QSK)¶

    (v1.24.2) How long the receiver stays muted after you stop keying — useful if you run an amplifier or relay that needs the receiver quiet for the whole transmission rather than switching between every dit and dah. This lives in the settings drawer, not on the CW page itself: see Semi QSK under Settings → Radio for the full set of modes and the delay control.

    Memory messages¶

    Six slots, each with macros expanded when you send:

    Macro Becomes
    {MYCALL} Your callsign from Settings
    {PARK} The park reference from the Park button
    {CALL} The station you are working
    {RST} A pro-forma signal report: 599, or 5NN if you long-press the {RST} button when inserting it (toggles a saved cut-numbers preference)
    {SERIAL} Your contest serial, incrementing with each exchange sent, with cut numbers (0 sent as T) — see Contest logging

    Sending and editing:

    • Tap a memory to send it. The button lights light grey while your finger is down so you can see which one will fire.
    • Drag up or down without lifting to move to a different button — useful because the buttons are narrow and a fingertip covers more than one. Lift on the one you want.
    • Hold for 800 ms and the editor opens immediately, with your finger still down. The hold timer restarts whenever you slide to a different button, so taking your time to aim never opens the editor by accident.
    • Slide sideways about 40 px and the touch is abandoned: nothing is sent (v1.26.0). The left-edge grip lies in front of the first three buttons, so a swipe that started there used to send a macro. A slide to the right from near the left edge now opens the page picker instead.
    • An empty slot opens the editor when tapped.

    STOP and the send queue¶

    (v1.26.0) A message tapped while another is keying is queued behind it, up to 256 characters. The Tab5 hands the radio the next part, 80 characters at most, only when the radio's own buffer is empty.

    While text is queued, the status line becomes a red button: STOP - drop N queued. It drops the text the radio has not been given yet. The part the radio already holds still runs to the end, because the QMX has no command to stop it.

    Each send is also checked against the band plan at the moment it is keyed. Outside the band, or with no IARU region set, the send is refused with a CW not sent: message and the queue is dropped. See Transmitting outside the band plan.

    The editor shows all six messages at once, so you can set up a whole activation in one visit.

    Macro sets¶

    The toggle at the bottom of the panel switches between two independent sets of six macros, each with its own editor:

    • General — everyday CQ/exchange messages. The default macros use 599 for the signal report.
    • Contest — the same six slots, with 5NN in place of 599. Whichever signal report you actually sent is scanned from the text at send time, so the log always records what went out, not a hard-coded assumption.

    Edit either set by holding a memory button as above — the editor's title names which set you are editing, and a toggle tap elsewhere mid-edit cannot redirect where Save writes to.

    Reading the band¶

    The decode ticker shows what the QMX's own CW decoder hears, letter by letter as it arrives.

    With no signal, a CW decoder produces a steady dribble of single characters — T T T T T. The ticker squelches these: when the same lone letter repeats, it is shown twice and then suppressed until something else arrives. A lone R, K or N, and EE, are never filtered, because those are real. The filter only affects what is displayed — callsign capture and the hints always see the raw stream, so nothing can be lost to it.

    The copilot hint line sits just above the ticker and says what the copy meant, in plain language:

    You see It means
    W1AW is calling CQ — tap the card to answer Someone is calling and you may answer
    W1AW is activating a park — tap the card to work them A POTA activation (CQ POTA)
    W1AW is answering YOU — tap the card to work them They came back to your call
    That was YOUR callsign Your call went by
    That was a signal report: 599 A report was sent
    That was their park reference: US-1211 A POTA reference was sent
    They are signing off (73 / TU) The contact is ending
    They want a repeat — send it again AGN / PSE
    QRZ — they are asking who called

    Hints are green when there is something to act on and amber otherwise, and they clear after about 25 seconds so a stale reading never sits beside newer copy.

    This is what makes the page usable if you cannot yet copy CW by ear: the ticker gives you the letters, the hint tells you what just happened, and NEXT tells you what to send back.

    The station card appears when a callsign is heard, showing the country, whether you have worked them before (and on this band), the report they sent you, and their park reference if they gave one. Tap the card to start working them; tap again to drop them.

    With nothing heard, the card shows "tap to enter a callsign" (v1.24.2) — tap it to type one on a popup keyboard instead of waiting for it to be decoded. That is how you adopt a station you can copy by ear but the decoder has not caught, or one you already know from another source.

    The pencil icon beside the card (v1.24.2) always opens the same manual-entry keyboard, whether or not a callsign is already showing — so a wrongly auto-adopted call can be corrected without first dropping the contact.

    The Log button above the card (v1.26.0) opens the Log a contact window with the current station's callsign filled in - one tap, where it used to be a trip through the settings drawer. With no station it opens the same window empty. The pencil icon sits one row above it.

    Listening in a browser? From v1.26.0 the web page has noise reduction for CW. It uses the spectral kind, which keeps a CW note. See Web DSP.

    While you transmit, the waterfall adds no rows (v1.26.0) - including when you key by hand - so your own signal is not painted over the band. It resumes about 0.7 seconds after the last element. The spectrum trace keeps drawing. The browser's waterfall does not do this yet. This is implemented and not yet measured against a hand key (Bryan N0LUF).

    Listening: the two audio paths, and what each one can do¶

    The Tab5 and the QMX produce separate audio from the same receiver, and knowing which is which is the difference between a setup that works first time and an evening of fighting it.

    QMX headphone output Tab5 output (speaker or headphones)
    Source the QMX's own demodulator the raw I/Q stream over USB
    Carries your keying sidetone yes no, and it never will
    Stereo separation of two stations no yes — Binaural CW
    Set its level with the radio's volume (or QMX volume in the drawer) RX Volume in Resource Management

    The two volumes are completely independent. Measured on the bench 2026-11-02: moving the QMX's AF gain across its whole useful range — 0 dB, then 40 dB, a factor of 100 in amplitude, each confirmed by the radio's own read-back — changed the level arriving over USB by nothing at all. I/Q mode carries the samples from before the QMX's audio chain, so the radio's volume and its RF gain both stay on the radio's side of the glass. Turn the QMX right down and the Tab5 keeps playing at exactly the same level.

    The Audio chapter covers the rest of the audio path — the AGC, the pan controls, and listening in a browser. What follows here is only what a CW operator has to decide.

    Why the Tab5 cannot give you a sidetone¶

    Its playback path is about 100 ms deep — ~80 ms of I2S buffering, up to 48.5 ms inside the codec write, plus a ~21 ms processing frame. A dot at 20 WPM lasts 60 ms, so the tone would begin after the element that produced it had already ended, and under QSK you would hear the previous character while sending the next. This is measured, it is the Tab5's own latency rather than the radio's, and no setting changes it. FlexRadio reach the same conclusion about their own hardware: the sidetone is delivered by whatever device is doing the keying.

    So your keying monitor always comes from the QMX.

    Binaural CW needs both ears¶

    Binaural CW (stereo separation) in Resource Management splits the CW passband the radio has already chosen into its lower and upper halves and pans them left and right, with a little cross-feed so a dead-centre station still sits in the middle. Two stations either side of your pitch then separate spatially instead of blurring into one note.

    Three things to know:

    • It needs RX Audio switched on first — the effect is produced on the Tab5.
    • It applies in CW and CW-R only. Every other mode is plain mono whatever this is set to.
    • ⛔ Do not split your headphones between the two radios while it is on. One ear then gives you one half of the passband and only a trace of the other, so you would lose stations on the far side of your own pitch. If you want the QMX in one ear and the Tab5 in the other, turn Binaural CW off and accept mono on the Tab5 side.

    The recommended setup for working stations by paddle¶

    Both ears on the Tab5 with Binaural CW on, and the QMX's sidetone switched into those same headphones on key-down — an analog audio switch driven from PTT. That is the only arrangement that gives you spatial separation while receiving and a monitor with correct QSK timing, because the switch moves the audio in hardware with no buffer in the way. The design is Michael KZ4LY's.

    Without a switch you have to choose one:

    • Both ears on the Tab5 — best receiving, no keying monitor.
    • QMX in one ear, Tab5 in the other, Binaural CW off — a monitor in one ear and mono receive in the other.

    (v1.25.0) The Tab5 now does this for you. Left at the factory setting of Relative, the QMX's main volume control moves the received audio and your sidetone together by the same amount, so you cannot bring the receive level down without taking your own monitor with it. Entering the CW page now checks the radio's reported Sidetone abs/rel setting, and if it reads Relative, switches it to Absolute and starts mirroring the QMX volume slider into Sidetone volume itself (with a 15% floor, so it can never go fully silent). Leaving the page restores Relative, so a standalone QMX is never left needing to be repaired by hand. If the radio is already Absolute — Michael KZ4LY's own deliberate binaural setup, described above — this does nothing at all.

    The CW monitor level row in the settings drawer still exists for setting the level and the abs/rel mode directly, and reads them back from the radio rather than keeping its own copy — useful outside the CW page, or if you want a level other than what the automatic mirror would pick.

    Running a QSO¶

    The NEXT button always shows exactly what it will send. One tap sends it and advances:

    1. CQ — sends your CQ memory. Stays here, listening.
    2. Report — once a station is adopted, sends <their call> plus your report memory.
    3. TU + log — sends your closing memory and writes the QSO to the ADIF log.

    A station is adopted automatically when the decoder hears your call followed by DE <their call> shortly after your CQ. You can also adopt anyone by tapping the station card, which is how you answer someone else's CQ. Long-press NEXT to drop the contact and go back to CQ.

    The logged contact records mode CW, the report you sent, and the report you received only if it was actually decoded — a report that was never exchanged is left out of the log rather than invented.

    Contest logging¶

    (v1.25.0) Set contest…, from the web CW panel (see Web UI → CW Control) or [cw] contest = in a config file, names the contest you are currently running. While it is set, every CW QSO logged here carries that name as ADIF CONTEST_ID, alongside a genuine contest exchange:

    • The serial you actually sent — read back from the expanded {SERIAL} macro text, never a number assumed from a counter — as ADIF STX_STRING.
    • The serial the other station sent back — captured from the decoder right after their report, the same way the report itself is captured — as ADIF SRX_STRING.

    The station card shows the received serial next to the report once it has been decoded. This is mutually exclusive with ARRL Field Day logging, which keeps writing its own CONTEST_ID the way it always has.

    Practice mode (no radio needed)¶

    Settings → CW Simulation Mode (or a three-finger hold on this page) turns it into a practice bench. A red border around the screen shows it is active, and the radio is never keyed while it is on — the status line says "Simulated:" rather than "Keying:" so a glance always tells you which is happening.

    The simulated band behaves like a real one:

    • Stations call CQ on their own every half-minute or so, some of them park activations, so you can practise answering rather than only being answered.
    • The copy is imperfect. Characters are dropped and mis-timed into neighbouring letters — E becomes I, T becomes M — exactly the way a real decoder fails. Callsigns are usually still readable because real CW repeats them, which is the skill the page is teaching.
    • The band has noise between transmissions.
    • Whoever you are working answers you through the whole exchange, and the QSO logs to ADIF like any other.

    Practice contacts are logged with no frequency, which marks them as test records — the ADIF viewer's "Del N test" button removes them in two taps when you are done.

    Turning simulation off clears the ticker, the hint, the station card and the ladder, so no phantom contact is left looking like real copy.

    Gestures¶

    The spectrum and waterfall behind the pane take the same touch gestures as the Panadapter page: pinch with two fingers to zoom and pan together (pan comes from how far the midpoint between your fingers moves, so a drag with roughly steady finger spacing pans without zooming much), and a one-finger swipe to pan without retuning. A three-finger hold toggles CW Simulation Mode on or off, the same gesture FT8 uses for its own simulator.

    Known limitations¶

    • No abort for a message the radio already holds (see the warning at the top) — both tested candidates (RX;, a mode bounce) were tried and neither works. Text still queued on the Tab5 can be dropped with STOP.
    • Zero-beat tuning aid and speed matching are designed but not yet built; both need real signals to develop against.
